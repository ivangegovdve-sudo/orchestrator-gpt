An essay by Ivan Gegov

A Chair or a Ladder

What AI trains us to become.

Ivan Gegov

A chair and a ladder can be made from the same oak. Both support weight. Both are useful. But they invite opposite postures.

A chair holds you where you are.

A ladder helps you reach a place you cannot yet stand—and can remain behind for the next person.

AI can be either.

It can remove drudgery, widen access, and give ordinary people capabilities that once belonged only to specialists. But every act of delegation also trains a habit. Some uses leave us with more time and more reach. Others leave us less able to explain our own work, challenge a result, or leave the platform that produced it.

The question is not whether AI will be used. It already is. Nor is the question whether convenience is bad. It is not.

The real question is simpler: after using this tool, am I more capable of judgment, or more dependent on judgment I cannot see?

The chair is not evil. On a hard day, convenience can be mercy. It can draft the email, translate the form, summarize the meeting, or turn a blank page into a beginning.

The danger begins not when you sit, but when you stop noticing what is being transferred along with the effort.

A tool that starts by helping can end up quietly governing. It drafts your message; soon it filters which messages count as urgent. It summarizes the discussion; soon it shapes what gets discussed at all. It remembers on your behalf—and there the pattern breaks, because you will never notice the thing you were not reminded of.

The issue is not saved labor. A calculator saves labor. So does a washing machine. The issue is whether the tool removes repetition while leaving judgment visible—or replaces judgment so seamlessly that we stop noticing it has been replaced.

Consider an analyst who prepares the same weekly report.

In the chair version, the analyst uploads the data and asks AI to identify what matters, explain the numbers, and recommend what the team should do. The report arrives faster and sounds polished. But its categories, assumptions, and omissions are hidden inside the system. The team receives an answer, not a method. When a conclusion is wrong, nobody can reconstruct how it was reached.

In the ladder version, the analyst defines the important measures first. AI flags anomalies and drafts the repetitive sections. Every claim links back to source data. The analyst checks a sample, records the system's misses, and shares the checklist with colleagues. A colleague improves it. The next person starts from a higher rung.

The same tool performed the same broad task. The difference was not technical skill. It was whether the human preserved intention, verification, and the ability to pass the method on.

That is what makes a ladder.

And it matters that this essay is itself an example: Ivan Gegov supplied the governing image and the argument; AI systems drafted and revised the prose. The question is not whether assistance was used. It is whether the result leaves behind something inspectable—something another mind can critique, adapt, or carry forward without being trapped inside the same tool.

The objection that should stop you

So far this sounds like a matter of personal discipline: be careful, verify, don't outsource your mind. That is the comforting version, and it is incomplete—because the strongest force pushing us toward the chair is not laziness. It is arithmetic.

If your employer counts output per hour, the ladder version of the analyst's work is irrational. Defining measures, checking samples, and writing down what the system missed takes longer. The analyst who does it files fewer reports than the analyst who does not. Not worse reports. Fewer. Over two quarters that gap shows up in a review, and the person who built the ladder is the one explaining themselves.

So let us say it plainly, because a sermon about responsibility will not beat a spreadsheet of hourly metrics: under that measurement, the chair is not a temptation. It is the correct move. Most people will take it, and they will be right to.

But look at what actually happened, because the verification did not stop being worth doing.

Both analysts hand in a report. Only one of them can say how it was made. When a conclusion turns out to be wrong—and one will—the cost does not land on the analyst who skipped the checking. It lands months later on whoever acted on it, with no way to reconstruct where it broke. The work did not disappear. It moved: off the ledger of the person who could still see it, onto the ledger of an organisation that cannot.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is not a story about weak will. Judgment is not being defeated by convenience. It is being outbid by a measurement that does not know it exists.

Which tells you what the remedy has to be. Not more discipline—nobody can verify everything, and exhaustion is not a moral failure. The remedy is anything that puts the cost back where somebody can see it.

Making judgment legible

That is the principle, and it is cheaper than it sounds: make judgment legible. If an AI system influences a decision, the path from input to outcome must be available at the level the stakes demand. Nobody needs to read model internals. People do need to know what data was used, what was ignored, what was considered and rejected, and where the system tends to fail.

Legibility is not an aesthetic preference. It is the difference between borrowing a capability and surrendering the ability to leave.

This is why portability matters: exportable records, open formats, preserved sources, audit trails that travel with the work rather than living inside one vendor's interface. When your notes, drafts, citations, and decision logs can only be read inside a single system, you have not adopted a tool. You have rented a mind-extension with moving-out fees.

The early internet is a compressed lesson. Open protocols made it normal to switch providers without losing the ability to communicate. Later, many platforms made identity, audience, history, and livelihood difficult to move. Both phases delivered convenience. Only one made departure survivable. AI will follow the same path unless the norms are set now, before defaults harden into infrastructure.

But "insist" cannot mean "hope individuals behave heroically." It has to mean: align incentives so that ladders are not career suicide.

What does that look like in practice?

In workplaces:

  • Measure quality and reversibility, not only speed. A "faster" report that cannot be audited is a hidden liability. Organizations already know how to pay for safety in other domains: checklists in aviation, code review in software, separation of duties in finance. AI-assisted work needs the equivalent.
  • Budget time for verification and documentation as part of the deliverable, not as a personal hobby done after hours. If a team wants reliability, it must purchase it in hours.
  • Require decision records that outlive the vendor: linked sources, rationale, and a minimal audit trail. The point is not bureaucracy; it is continuity.

In schools:

  • Teach verification, sourcing, and method—not just "prompting." If students learn that the skill is producing plausible text, the chair wins by design. If they learn to trace claims, compare answers, and document reasoning, the ladder becomes part of competence.

In organizations that buy AI systems:

  • Treat portability and logging as procurement requirements. If a vendor cannot provide exportable work artifacts, citations, or review pathways, the product is not "streamlined"—it is sticky.
  • Make human override and appeal real. If an AI triages, ranks, or flags, someone must be accountable for contesting it without needing permission from the model.

For developers and toolmakers:

  • Build ladder features into the default experience: sources attached to claims, versioned prompts or instructions, reproducible runs where possible, and "show your work" modes that are not hidden behind an expert toggle.
  • Reduce the time-cost of doing the right thing. The chair wins when the ladder feels like extra paperwork. Tools can reverse that by making verification fast and documentation automatic.

For governments and regulators:

  • Protect rights that convenience otherwise erases: the ability to obtain records, to understand the basis of consequential decisions, to appeal to a human, and to move one's data and work artifacts.
  • Focus not only on model capability but on deployment conditions: logging, auditability, and recourse. Many harms come less from "AI being smart" than from AI being unchallengeable.

None of this is utopian. It is ordinary institutional design, and none of it is new. We already accept that some domains require friction: food labels, financial disclosures, safety inspections, breach notices. Each is a deliberate cost, imposed because the alternative cost was invisible until it arrived. AI is a general-purpose system for producing outputs that look authoritative. The minimum viable guardrail is to ensure that authority can be questioned without begging the system that generated it.

Where that leaves you

Individual discipline is not a substitute for any of that. But individual action is not nothing either, and the reason is narrower and better than encouragement.

A record of where the system failed is not self-improvement; it is evidence. A method your colleagues can inspect is not generosity; it is the difference between a habit, which lives and dies with you, and a fact, which can be pointed at in a meeting. Metrics change when the thing they ignore becomes too visible to keep ignoring. A ladder only you can climb changes nothing. A ladder three people use is an argument.

That is also the honest limit of it. One analyst documenting misses does not move a company, and anyone promising you more than that is selling something. It is a small act with a real mechanism, which is a different thing from a solution.

Sometimes the act is refusal: a teacher who rejects automated surveillance, a worker who demands a human appeal, a team that insists on citations rather than vibes. These are not purity tests. They are attempts to keep judgment in view. More often the practical move is not "ban the tool" but "use it in a way that leaves evidence."

The real divide is not between adopters and skeptics. It is between accepting a default and deliberately shaping what happens next—especially when the default is optimised for speed and lock-in at the expense of legibility.

Before delegating, ask (as a person or as a team)

  1. Am I handing over repetition, or judgment?
  2. Can I explain and verify the result at the level the stakes demand?
  3. If this provider disappeared tomorrow, would I still possess my work, sources, and method?
  4. Who pays the time-cost of verification—and have we budgeted for it honestly?
  5. What can I leave behind, in a form someone else can inspect, so the next person starts higher?

Use the best tools available. Let them make you faster. But treat the provider as replaceable and your own understanding as the infrastructure you cannot outsource.

Learn enough to know when the machine is wrong—and insist that your environment gives you the time and the standing to say so.

Delegate effort without delegating responsibility.

Keep enough friction to feel the weight of judgment.

Write down what the machine got wrong, where someone else can find it.

AI will be used. The open question is what kind of people its use will cultivate, and what kind of institutions those people will be able to keep.

Sit when you need to; the chair was built for a reason, and some days it is the only honest option. But know when you are sitting, and know what put you there—and don't mistake enforced sitting for comfort. Stand where the decision matters. And when you climb, leave the ladder where someone else can reach it: not as a kindness, but because a ladder nobody else can find gets counted as nothing at all—and then push for a world where climbing is not punished as inefficiency.
