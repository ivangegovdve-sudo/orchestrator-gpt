An essay by Ivan Gegov

A Chair or a Ladder

What AI trains us to become.

Ivan Gegov

A chair and a ladder can be made from the same oak. Both hold weight. Both are useful. But they ask for opposite postures.

A chair holds you where you are. A ladder helps you reach a place you cannot yet stand — and stays behind for the next person. AI can be either.

It removes drudgery, widens access, and hands ordinary people capabilities that once belonged to specialists. But every act of delegation also trains a habit. Some uses leave us with more time and more reach. Others leave us less able to explain our own work, question a result, or walk away from the platform that produced it.

AI will be used; it already is. Convenience isn't the problem either. The real question is narrower: after using this tool, am I better at judgment, or more dependent on judgment I can't see?

On a hard day, convenience is mercy. It drafts the email, translates the form, summarizes the meeting, turns a blank page into a beginning. The trouble starts when you stop noticing what leaves with the effort.

A tool that begins by helping can end up quietly governing. It drafts your message, then filters which messages count as urgent. It summarizes the discussion, then shapes what gets discussed at all. It remembers for you — and there the pattern breaks, because you never notice the thing you weren't reminded of.

A calculator saves labor. So does a washing machine. What matters is whether the tool removes repetition and leaves your judgment visible, or replaces it so smoothly you stop noticing it's gone.

Consider an analyst who prepares the same weekly report. In the chair version, she uploads the data and asks AI to say what matters, explain the numbers, and recommend what the team should do. The report arrives fast and sounds polished. Its categories, assumptions, and omissions stay hidden inside the system. The team gets an answer, not a method. When a conclusion is wrong, nobody can reconstruct how it was reached.

In the ladder version, she defines the measures that matter first. AI flags anomalies and drafts the repetitive sections. Every claim links back to source data. She checks a sample, records the system's misses, and shares the checklist. A colleague improves it. The next person starts from a higher rung.

Same tool, same work. The difference was whether the human kept intention, verification, and the ability to pass the method on. That is what makes a ladder.

This essay is itself the example: Ivan Gegov supplied the governing image and the argument; AI drafted and revised the prose. What matters is that the result leaves something inspectable — something another mind can critique, adapt, or carry forward without being trapped inside the same tool.

The objection that should stop you

So far this sounds like personal discipline: be careful, verify, don't outsource your mind. That's the comforting version, and it's incomplete. The strongest force pushing us toward the chair isn't laziness. It's arithmetic.

If your employer counts output per hour, the ladder version of the work is irrational. Defining measures, checking samples, writing down what the system missed — all of it takes longer. The analyst who does it files fewer reports than the analyst who doesn't. Not worse reports. Fewer. Over two quarters that gap shows up in a review, and the person who built the ladder is the one explaining herself.

A sermon about responsibility won't beat a spreadsheet of hourly metrics. Under that measurement the chair is the correct move. Most people will take it, and they'll be right to.

But look at what actually happened. Both analysts hand in a report. Only one of them can say how it was made. When a conclusion turns out wrong — and one will — the cost doesn't land on the analyst who skipped the checking. It lands months later on whoever acted on it, with no way to trace where it broke. The work didn't disappear. It moved: off the ledger of the person who could still see it, onto the ledger of an organisation that can't.

That's the whole mechanism, and it isn't about weak will. Judgment isn't being defeated by convenience. It's being outbid by a measurement that doesn't know it exists.

So the remedy can't be more discipline. Nobody can verify everything, and exhaustion isn't a moral failure. The remedy is anything that puts the cost back where someone can see it.

Making judgment legible

That's the principle, and it's cheaper than it sounds. If an AI system shapes a decision, the path from input to outcome has to be available at the level the stakes demand. Nobody needs to read model internals. People do need to know what data was used, what was ignored, what was considered and rejected, and where the system tends to fail.

Legibility is the difference between borrowing a capability and surrendering the ability to leave. That's why portability matters: exportable records, open formats, preserved sources, audit trails that travel with the work instead of living inside one vendor's interface. When your notes, drafts, citations, and decision logs can only be read inside a single system, you haven't adopted a tool. You've rented a mind-extension with moving-out fees.

The early internet is a compressed lesson. Open protocols made it normal to switch providers without losing the ability to communicate. Later, platforms made identity, audience, history, and livelihood hard to move. Both delivered convenience. Only one made leaving survivable. AI goes the same way unless the norms are set now, before defaults harden into infrastructure. And setting norms can't mean hoping people behave heroically. It means aligning incentives so that building a ladder isn't career suicide.

What does that look like in practice? In workplaces: measure quality and reversibility, not only speed — a faster report that can't be audited is a hidden liability, and we already pay for safety this way elsewhere, with checklists in aviation, code review in software, separation of duties in finance. Budget verification into the deliverable instead of leaving it for unpaid overtime. Keep decision records that outlive the vendor — linked sources, rationale, a minimal audit trail — for continuity, not bureaucracy. In schools: teach verification, sourcing, and method, not just prompting; if students learn the skill is producing plausible text, the chair wins by design. In organizations that buy AI: make portability and logging procurement requirements — a vendor who can't hand back your artifacts, citations, and review paths is selling lock-in, not efficiency — and make human override real. For toolmakers: put the ladder in the default — sources attached to claims, versioned prompts, "show your work" that isn't buried behind a toggle — and make doing the right thing fast instead of extra paperwork. For regulators: protect the rights convenience erases, and watch how systems are deployed, not just how capable they are; most harm comes from AI being unchallengeable, not from AI being smart.

None of this is new. Food labels, financial disclosures, safety inspections, breach notices are all deliberate friction, added because the alternative cost stayed invisible until it arrived. AI produces output that looks authoritative. The least we can do is make sure that authority can be questioned without begging the system that produced it.

Where that leaves you

Individual discipline won't substitute for any of that. But individual action isn't nothing, for a narrow reason. A record of where the system failed is evidence. A method your colleagues can inspect outlives you — a habit dies with you, a written fact can be pointed at in a meeting. Metrics change when the thing they ignore becomes too visible to keep ignoring. A ladder only you can climb changes nothing. A ladder three people use is an argument.

That's the honest limit too. One analyst documenting misses doesn't move a company, and anyone promising you more is selling something. It's a small act with a real mechanism — not a solution. Sometimes the act is refusal: a teacher who rejects automated surveillance, a worker who demands a human appeal, a team that insists on citations instead of vibes. Not purity tests — ways to keep judgment in view. More often the move is simpler: use the tool in a way that leaves evidence. The real divide is between accepting a default and shaping what comes next — especially when the default is tuned for speed and lock-in.

Before delegating, ask: Am I handing over repetition, or judgment? Can I explain and verify the result at the level the stakes demand? If this provider vanished tomorrow, would I still have my work, sources, and method? Who pays the time-cost of verification — and have we budgeted for it honestly? What can I leave behind that someone else can inspect, so the next person starts higher?

Use the best tools there are. Let them make you faster. But treat the provider as replaceable and your own understanding as the infrastructure you can't outsource. Learn enough to know when the machine is wrong — and insist you have the time and the standing to say so. Delegate the effort, not the responsibility. Keep enough friction to feel the weight of judgment. Write down what the machine got wrong, where someone else can find it.

AI will be used. The open question is what kind of people its use will make, and what kind of institutions those people will be able to keep. Sit when you need to; the chair was built for a reason, and some days it's the only honest option. But know when you're sitting, and know what put you there. Stand where the decision matters. And when you climb, leave the ladder where the next person can reach it.
